# Update the book's standards, benchmarks and images

The enrichment work splits into five streams, and their priority order is clear. **First, edition hygiene.** At least ten standards the book cites, or should cite, issued new editions between 2023 and 2026. Several chapter passages now point to superseded documents: TIA-607-D, the 2010 IEC 62305 series, EN 50272-2, NFPA 2001-2022 and BS 1192-4. Some also attach the wrong source to a claim, for example the "~USD 9,000 per minute" downtime figure, which comes from a 2016 Ponemon/Emerson study and not from Uptime Institute. **Second, market data.** It can now be re-based on three 2026 publications: Turner & Townsend's Data Centre Construction Cost Index (DCCI) 2026-27, Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 Development Cost Guide and JLL's 2026 Outlook. Each measures a different scope, so the book must print a scope column beside every number. **Third, emerging topics.** Six of them change what a quantity surveyor (QS) measures: 800 VDC rack power, NFPA 855 lithium-ion thresholds, behind-the-meter bridging power, mandatory heat reuse in Germany, prefabricated eHouses, and immersion fluid as a separate bill of quantities (BOQ) item. Most of the numbers behind them are vendor claims, so the book should present the measurement consequence and leave the vendor's percentage out. **Fourth, the thin chapters.** Chapters 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17 can be expanded with sourced facts on aspirating smoke detection (ASD) classes, leak detection, TIA-942-C rating changes, EPMS time synchronisation, the SCL Protocol and FIDIC time and claims clauses. Much of that material is law-firm or manufacturer paraphrase and must be checked against the standards text before it reads as settled. **Fifth, images.** 42 Wikimedia Commons images were checked as public domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA and cover most of Volumes 2-4. Nothing openly licensed shows a coolant distribution unit (CDU), a cold plate, a lithium-ion UPS rack or white-space busway, so those need commissioned drawings. The source register (`wip/research/2026-11-findings.md`), the image register (`wip/research/image-register.md`) and the draft BibTeX (`wip/sources.bib`) carry every item below, each with its verification flag.

## At least ten cited standards changed edition between 2023 and 2026

The most visible errors are in Volume 3. Chapter 12 cites **TIA-607-D**. That edition was superseded by **ANSI/TIA-607-E (May 2024)**, which covers the bonding backbone (TBB/TMGB/TGB) and states that it does not override the NEC ([NSAI 607-E](https://shop.standards.ie/en-ie/standards/tia-ansi-tia-607-e-2024-1066069_saig_tia_tia_3441099)). The same chapter specifies IEC 62305 LPL I without an edition. The series is now at **Edition 3 (2024) for Parts 1-4**, and BSI advises adopting the four parts together because the Part 2 risk assessment drives the rest ([IEC 62305-1](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/27136); [BSI](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/protection-against-lightning-general-principles-3)). The LPL I paragraph also cites `@rics2021nrm2` for rolling-sphere and mesh parameters. NRM2 is a measurement standard and does not define those parameters, so the citation should move to `iec62305_1_2024`. Chapter 10 cites EN 50272-2 for battery-room hydrogen ventilation. **EN IEC 62485-2:2018 replaced it**, and it is the identical adoption of IEC 62485-2:2010 Ed. 1, which is still current ([IEC](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/7091); [NSAI](https://shop.standards.ie/en-ie/standards/en-iec-62485-2-2018-353464_saig_cenelec_cenelec_806241)). **IEC 60364-1 Ed. 6 (Sep 2025)** restructured and renumbered the 2005 text, so any clause number quoted in Chapter 11 needs re-checking ([IEC](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/63699)). The remaining electrical updates are **IEC 62271-200:2021 with AMD1:2024** for MV switchgear ([IEC](https://webstore.iec.ch/publication/63466)), **IEC 62040-3:2021**, whose efficiency annex is now normative and which serves as the UPS factory acceptance test (FAT) basis ([IEC](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/60140)), and **NFPA 70-2026** ([ANSI](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/nfpa/nfpa702026)). Under the 2026 Article 645, the emergency power off (EPO) requirement reportedly applies only where the alternative wiring allowances are used. That point comes from a forum post and must be verified before use.

Batteries and fire protection carry the most commercial weight. **UL 9540 is in its 3rd edition (2023)**. ULSE's release is dated 28 June 2023, and other sources say April 2023 ([ULSE](https://ulse.org/news/ulse-publishes-third-edition-ul-9540-energy-storage-systems-and-equipment)). **UL 9540A reached its 5th edition in March 2025**, and Intertek reports a 6th edition dated 13 March 2026 that has not yet been seen in the ULSE catalogue ([UL](https://www.ul.com/services/ul-9540a-test-method); [Intertek](https://www.intertek.com/blog/2026/03-23-understanding-the-2026-update-to-ul-9540a/)). **NFPA 2001 is current at the 2025 edition**, with TIA 25-1 (egress time study) effective 5 May 2026 ([ANSI](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/nfpa/nfpa20012025); [NFPA TIA](https://docinfofiles.nfpa.org/files/AboutTheCodes/2001/TIA_2001_25_1.pdf)). Chapter 13 needs that key: its 10-minute hold-time statement currently cites `@nfpa75`. **ISO 14520-1:2023** (4th edition) replaces the 2015 edition ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/79444.html)). **NFPA 76-2024** replaces 2020, but this was confirmed only from retailer listings ([NSAI](https://shop.standards.ie/en-ie/standards/nfpa-76-2024-829570_saig_nfpa_nfpa_3362064)). **EN 54-20:2006+AC:2008** is still the current ASD standard, because no revision has been published ([DIN Media](https://dinmedia.de/en/standard/din-en-54-20/115703955)). On the ICT and information side, **TIA-942-C (May 2024)** makes the Annex D rating tables normative and adds an informative immersion-cooling annex ([TIA](https://tiaonline.org/?p=35007)). **BS 1192-4 is withdrawn** and superseded by **BS EN ISO 19650-4:2022**, so Chapter 22's COBie section needs a new anchor ([BSI](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/products/collaborative-production-of-information-fulfilling-employer-s-information-exchange-requirements-using-cobie-code-of-practice)).

Three commercial documents also change. The **FIDIC Silver Book 2017 was reprinted in November 2022 with amendments**, effective 1 January 2023 under the same ISBN. The reprint clarifies "matter" versus "Claim" in Sub-Clause 3.5 ([Fenwick Elliott](https://www.fenwickelliott.com/knowledge-hub/international-quarterlies/issue-34/updating-the-fidic-rainbow-suite-the-2022-changes/)). The least disruptive fix is to keep `@fidic2017silver` and update its `edition` field. The draft BibTeX includes a separate `fidic2017silver_2022` entry only for use where a passage depends on the amended wording. The **RICS Whole Life Carbon Assessment (WLCA) 2nd edition** has been mandatory for RICS members since 1 July 2024 ([RICS](https://rics.org/news-insights/wlca-standard-2nd-edition-now-in-full-effect)). **RICS Cost Prediction** was reissued as a professional standard in June 2024 ([RICS](https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/construction-standards/rics-cost-prediction-professional-statement-global-1st-edition)). Together with **AACE RP 18R-97 (rev. 2020)** ([AACE](https://pathlms.com/aace/courses/2928/documents/3803)), Cost Prediction should replace the job-advert and Scribd `[REVIEW-SOURCE]` citations in Chapter 3. **ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019, ASHRAE/IES Standard 202-2024** and **CIBSE Code M (2022)** should do the same for the commissioning-process claims in Chapter 20 ([ASHRAE](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/ashrae/ansiashraeies2022024); [CIBSE](https://www.cibse.org/knowledge-research/knowledge-portal/commissioning-code-m-ccm-commissioning-management-2022)). The Level 1-5 model is industry convention and not defined by any single standard, and the book should say so. Five items remain UNVERIFIED and must be checked against the publisher before citation: the IEC 61439-2 edition, IEC 60364-5-52, the SCL Protocol catalogue page, the Incoterms 2020 page, and whether an NFPA 855-2026 edition exists.

## 2026 cost data runs from USD 9.2 to 18.3 per watt, but the scopes differ

The book's current Chapter 3 range of **USD 7.5-12.5M per MW** cites Linesight and the AECOM handbook. It can now be tested against primary 2026 data. **T&T DCCI 2026-27** (22 Oct 2026) covers 62 markets on a typical 60 MW design. It gives a **US average of USD 12.08/W**, **USD 14-16/W for advanced AI-specific US facilities**, **Tokyo at USD 18.28/W** as the most expensive market, and a **13% year-on-year rise** in North America and Europe ([T&T](https://www.turnerandtownsend.com/insights/data-centre-construction-cost-index-2026-27/)). Press coverage of the same index puts the **Middle East cheapest at USD 9.93/W on average**: Saudi Arabia USD 10.80, Qatar USD 9.80 and the UAE USD 9.20. These figures are for air-cooled designs and exclude land, utility works and IT ([IndexBox](https://www.indexbox.io/blog/middle-east-ranked-cheapest-region-for-data-centre-construction-as-ai-demand-surges/); [Enterprise AM](https://enterpriseam.com/menaplus/2026/10/28/gulf-leads-the-world-on-data-center-construction-costs-turner-townsend-says/)). The Gulf numbers were seen only in search snippets of press articles. Treat them as unverified until someone reads them in T&T's interactive report. Saudi Arabia is reported as both "40th of 62" and "23rd-cheapest". These are the same position counted from opposite ends.

The other two publishers define cost differently. **Cushman & Wakefield (Aug 2026)** gives **USD 17.6M/MW all-in greenfield** for North America, excluding chips, within a **USD 8.9-23.3M/MW** range. It reports a **21% rise since Q4 2024** and identifies power infrastructure as the largest line, at **21% of a greenfield build** ([IREI](https://irei.com/news/cushman-wakefield-releases-2026-data-center-development-cost-guide-citing-21-rise-in-per-mw-construction-costs/); [Finviz/Business Wire](https://finviz.com/news/388803/cushman-wakefield-releases-2026-data-center-development-cost-guide-citing-21-rise-in-per-mw-construction-costs)). **JLL** gives **USD 11.3M/MW for shell and core only** (2026 forecast, +6%), with AI tenant fit-out of **up to USD 25M/MW** on top ([JLL](https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/market-outlook/data-center-outlook)). The gap between USD 12 and USD 17.6 does not mean one source is wrong: one excludes power infrastructure and the other includes it. Chapter 3 should therefore show a benchmark table with a scope column and a normalisation step before any figure feeds a cost plan. That is the QS lesson these data teach. One more number is the researcher's own extrapolation and has no published source: applying T&T's roughly 10% US AI premium to the Gulf air-cooled baselines gives about USD 10.1-11.9/W. If the book uses it, it must be labelled as the author's calculation. No accessible source split cost into mechanical and electrical percentages, so Chapter 1's CapEx allocation section has no 2026 sourced split to adopt.

Lead times explain why Chapter 18 exists. Wood Mackenzie's Q2 2025 survey reports **power transformers at about 128 weeks, generator step-up (GSU) units at about 144 weeks and distribution transformers at about 30 weeks** ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2025/08/15/us-transformer-supply-may-fall-sharply-in-2025-says-wood-mackenzie/)). It also projects a **30% US power-transformer supply deficit in 2025**, with imports supplying about 80% ([Wood Mackenzie](https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/power-transformers-and-distribution-transformers-will-face-supply-deficits-of-30-and-10-in-2025)). NREL records transformer prices up **4-9 times in three years** ([NREL](https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy24osti/87653.pdf)). Generator, switchgear and UPS lead times come only from secondary and vendor sources, for example generators at 60-100 weeks per C&W as quoted by Terrapin ([Terrapin](https://terrapincg.com/news/switchgear-transformer-generator-lead-times-2026)), and they differ by two to three times between sources. The book can state the order on the critical path (transformers, then generators, then MV switchgear, then UPS) with confidence, but the week counts for everything below transformers are directional only. No 2024-2026 source gave chiller or CDU lead times.

Two figures in the book are older or weaker than the text implies. The **USD 9,000/minute** downtime figure in Chapter 1 and the introduction is **USD 8,851/min** from Ponemon/Emerson (2016, 63 US sites) ([Emerson](https://emerson.com/en-us/news/corporate/network-power-study)). It is not an Uptime figure. It should be re-attributed and placed alongside Uptime's **2026 Annual Outage Analysis**: **57% of respondents said their latest major outage cost more than USD 100,000**, and about **1 in 5 said more than USD 1M** for the second year running ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260513289344/en/Uptime-Announces-Annual-Outage-Analysis-Report-2026)). The second is Chapter 20's claim that commissioning costs **8-12% of total CapEx**, against 1-2% for commercial buildings. The only rigorous benchmark found is **LBNL's median of 0.4% of construction cost** for new-building commissioning ([LBNL](https://cx.lbl.gov/cost-benefit.html); [Mills 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12053-011-9116-8)). The only data-centre figure is a vendor engineer's "ideal" of **about 1.5%** ([DCK](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/business/getting-the-most-out-of-commissioning-through-best-practices)). Both sources are far below 8-12%. Either the chapter's figure counts items the others leave out (FAT travel, flushing, load-bank hire, test fuel and energy), or it is inflated. The book should define what the percentage includes, or drop the number.

## Six emerging topics change what the quantity surveyor measures

**800 VDC power** is the most disruptive change for Chapter 11 and the one with the newest sources. NVIDIA's architecture converts 13.8 kV AC to 800 VDC at the facility perimeter for 1 MW racks from 2027, and NVIDIA claims about 45% less copper and up to 5% better efficiency ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-800-v-hvdc-architecture-will-power-the-next-generation-of-ai-factories)). The 45% figure is a vendor claim, and NVIDIA's own "85% more power" and later "157% more power" statements conflict. The neutral anchor is the **OCP Mt Diablo Disaggregated Power Rack Specification v1.0.0 (8 Oct 2026)** from Microsoft, Meta and Google. It moves AC/DC conversion, protection and optional storage into a sidecar rack that feeds ±400 VDC to IT racks of 100 kW to 1 MW ([OCP](https://www.opencompute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/OCP-Specification-Mt.-Diablo-Disaggregated-Power-Rack-Specification-v1.0.0.pdf)). For the QS, fewer AC switchboards and PDU/RPP items are replaced by DC busway, protection at every boundary (power room to hall, hall to row, row to rack), and copper tonnage as the main quantity driver. The demarcation between owner-furnished (OFCI) and contractor-furnished (CFCI) equipment also moves to the sidecar input terminals, and Chapter 2's RACI matrix should show that. Code treatment of 800 VDC under NFPA 70 and IEC 60364 is unsettled. That supports design-liability carve-outs or provisional sums in Chapter 19.

**NFPA 855** applies once **lithium-ion ESS exceed 20 kWh aggregate per fire area**, measured on nameplate energy and not usable energy ([ACP](https://cleanpower.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/ACP-ES-Product-6-ESS-Codes-and-Standards-Overview-6.28.23.pdf)), and this brings most lithium-ion UPS rooms into scope. UL 9540A data is needed above 50 kWh per group, for spacing under 0.9 m, or above the 600 kWh Table 9.4.1 maximum. That trigger comes from ZincFive, a battery vendor, and was seen as a snippet ([ZincFive](https://zincfive.com/blog/ensuring-ess-safety-in-data-centers-with-nfpa-855-part-2/)). Chapter 10 should therefore add fire-rated room area, explosion control, gas detection and UL 9540A reports as measured or submittal items. Microsoft's Dublin site, with Eaton UPS and Enel X aggregation, shows a grid-interactive UPS selling fast frequency response, but no capacity was disclosed ([Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/sustainability/ireland-wind-farm-datacenter-ups/)). The book can use it as a contract-structure example (revenue share, degradation, resilience risk) but cannot use it as a cost benchmark.

**Bridging power** has become a real Chapter 9 topic. Jefferies estimates **8-10 GW of data-centre gas turbines through 2030**, VoltaGrid will supply **2.3 GW behind the meter for Oracle/Stargate**, and Brookfield plans **USD 5B for Bloom fuel cells** ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2025/10/data-center-developers-turn-to-distributed-behind-the-meter-power-94174247)). Developers describe these "bridge" assets becoming permanent ([DCF](https://www.datacenterfrontier.com/energy/article/55403280/power-first-ai-data-centers-become-energy-systems)). The QS implication is an interface package and not a unit rate. That package covers MV synchronisation, gas regulating stations, emissions plant, and a demobilisation provisional sum for when the grid connection arrives. No installed USD/kW for these assets was found.

**Heat reuse** is where regulation already sets quantities. **Directive (EU) 2023/1791** (OJ L 231, 20 Sep 2023) requires reporting from data centres of 500 kW or more of IT power demand. It also requires data centres above 1 MW of total rated energy input to use waste heat unless that is not feasible ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2023/1791/oj/eng)). The article text was not read, and secondary sources disagree on whether the test is "technically and economically" or "technically or economically", and on whether it sits in Art. 26(6) or 26(7). **German EnEfG § 11**, read from the official statute site, is stricter. New data centres need **PUE ≤ 1.2** and an **energy reuse factor of 10/15/20%** for start of operation from July 2026/2027/2028. They also need **100% renewable electricity from 1 Jan 2027**, and the exemptions depend on who builds the heat-network connection line ([gesetze-im-internet.de](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/enefg/__11.html)). A 24 June 2026 draft amendment relaxes PUE for existing sites but keeps the reuse minimums ([Gleiss Lutz](https://www.gleisslutz.com/en/know-how/data-centre-update-draft-bill-amend-energy-efficiency-act)). Meta's Odense scheme is a worked Nordic example (about 45 MW of heat via ammonia heat pumps), but its MWh and household figures conflict between Ramboll and TechRadar ([Ramboll](https://www.ramboll.com/projects/energy/meta-surplus-heat-to-district-heating)). These requirements belong in Chapter 1 (performance metrics), Chapter 5 (heat-recovery plant and the boundary heat transfer station) and Chapter 7 (warm liquid return as a value-engineering link).

**Prefabrication and immersion** share one lesson: vendor savings claims are weaker than the measurement consequences. Schneider WP 218 finds only about **2% capex saving** for prefabricated against traditional at 440 kW ([Schneider](https://download.schneider-electric.com/doc/SPD_VAVR-9U4PPA_EN/WP218R0.pdf)). The larger figures (30% TCO, 50% lead time, 80% site-hours) are vendor or sponsored claims. The QS issues are a lump sum per skid without unit-rate transparency, FAT as a payment milestone, and vesting bonds for off-site goods. Those belong in Chapters 2, 18 and 19. For immersion, one unnamed study cited by Data Center Knowledge puts capex at **about USD 1.0M/MW against USD 0.65M/MW for direct-to-chip (DTC)**, seen as a snippet ([DCK](https://datacenterknowledge.com/cooling/why-immersion-cooling-still-hasn-t-gone-mainstream)). A chemicals seller prices **dielectric fluid at USD 3-12/L against USD 0.15-0.40/L for glycol** ([Alliance Chemical](https://alliancechemical.com/blogs/articles/direct-to-chip-vs-immersion-cooling-fluid-types)). Even as indicative numbers, they justify measuring first-fill fluid in litres as its own BOQ item in Chapter 7. **Embodied carbon** connects to the BOQ through RICS WLCA (aligned with ICMS 3) and the OCP/iMasons Embodied Carbon Disclosure specification (Oct 2025) ([OCP](https://www.opencompute.org/blog/open-compute-project-foundation-and-imasons-develop-taxonomy-for-carbon-disclosure)). That argues for requiring environmental product declarations (EPDs) in Chapter 18 vendor packages. **GCC specifics** remain thin. Primary sources found are DEWA's slab-based connection pricing, under which over-declared load raises cost, and its 2021 Major Project Guidelines ([DEWA](https://www.dewa.gov.ae/-/media/Files/Suppliers-and-Partners/Major-Project-Guidelines--Electricity--Jun-2021.ashx)), plus a 2026 MCIT incentive catalogue ([MCIT](https://www.mcit.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2026-08/AI%20Service%20Catalog%20MCIT%202026.pdf)). The texts of SBC 801 and the UAE fire code data-centre provisions were not retrieved.

## The thin chapters gain measurable facts, mostly from secondary sources

Chapter 14 (fire detection) can explain why ASD classes affect cost. EN 54-20 classes A, B and C apply to the whole sampling system as installed. One vendor example rates the same detector **Class A at 16 holes and Class C at 72 holes**, so a specification that demands Class A at every hole caps the coverage per detector and raises the detector count ([Xtralis](https://xtralis.com/file/539); [Eurofyre](https://eurofyre.co.uk/?p=4063)). The transport-time limits are **120 s under NFPA 72**, and **90 s for early warning and 60 s for very early warning** are commonly attributed to NFPA 76. Those attributions come from a forum thread and a Scribd application guide ([Eng-Tips](https://eng-tips.com/threads/air-sampling-systems-transport-time.244198)), so they must be checked against NFPA 72-2025 and NFPA 76-2024 before they appear in prose. Chapter 15 can draw on the OCP cold-plate leak detection white paper (CDU, manifold and quick-disconnect zones, with automatic isolation by solenoid valve) ([OCP](https://opencompute.org/documents/acs-cold-plate-leak-detection-and-intervention-white-paper-pdf-1)). It can also use ASHRAE TC 9.9's bulletin recommending lab-verified coolant quality at first fill and at intervals, a companion to its 2024 cold-plate resiliency guidance ([StorageReview](https://www.storagereview.com/news/ashrae-tc-9-9-flags-four-ways-direct-to-chip-cooling-loops-go-wrong)). That supports a provisional sum for periodic sampling and a defined OFCI/CFCI line for fill and passivation. No citable NFPA 75 drainage provision was found.

Chapter 16 should state that TIA-942-C keeps four rating levels (Rated-3 concurrently maintainable, Rated-4 fault tolerant) and adds a minimum of two fibres for horizontal backbone cabling, floor-loading and minimum cabinet-width provisions. An AI addendum is under way ([DCF](https://www.datacenterfrontier.com/design/article/33037194/c-revision-of-tia-942-data-center-standard-specifies-for-fiber-connectivity-cabinet-widths); [TIA](https://tiaonline.org/products/tia-942/)). The defined spaces (ER, MDA, IDA, HDA, ZDA, EDA) make natural BOQ headings. Chapter 17 gains a defensible technical point. EPMS records events to the millisecond while a BMS responds in 5-60 s, so sequence-of-events correlation needs **IEEE 1588 PTP with the IEEE C37.238 power profile**. That puts GPS grandmaster clocks and PTP-aware switches in the BOQ ([Siemens](https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:85039708-2b45-4081-bffb-3bc09af0b642/Data-Centers-RefArch-Single-UI-BMSEPMS_original.pdf); [Cyber Sciences/Trystar](https://trystar.com/wp-content/uploads/PP-PTP-01_EPMS-HD_presentation.pdf)). No published points-per-MW norm exists, so the book should tell the QS to build point counts from the device schedule. The metering-tier convention and the IEC 62443 cost effects are UNVERIFIED background only.

Chapter 19 needs the most correction. The **SCL Protocol 2nd edition (Feb 2017)** dropped the 1st edition's preference for time impact analysis. For after-the-event analysis it names no preferred method, and it lists selection factors instead. It defines concurrency as an Employer Risk Event and a Contractor Risk Event that are both effective causes of critical delay at the same time. It allows an EOT only after available float is used up ([Fenwick Elliott](https://www.fenwickelliott.com/research-insight/newsletters/international-quarterly/scl-delay-disruption-protocol-second-edition)). AACE RP 29R-03 is a taxonomy of observational and modeled methods with nine implementation protocols, and it ranks none of them ([AACE ToC](https://web.aacei.org/docs/default-source/toc/toc_29r-03.pdf?sfvrsn=4)). On FIDIC, Silver Book **8.5 is EOT and 8.8 is Delay Damages** ([Emerald preview](https://bookstore.emerald.com/media/preview/9781836081814-23-2.pdf)). The **Notice of Claim falls due 28 days after awareness and the fully detailed Claim within 84 days** (42 days under the 1999 forms). Under the Silver Book these go to the other Party, because there is no Engineer ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/new-fidic-second-editions-revamped-claims-and-dispute-resolution-provisions)). Concurrency is left to the Special Provisions, and outcomes depend on the governing law: *Walter Lilly* gives a full EOT, while *City Inn* and civil-law systems apportion ([Fenwick Elliott](https://www.fenwickelliott.com/research-insight/newsletters/international-quarterly/how-fidic-contract-deals-with-time)). The chapter's "Sub-Clause 8.4/8.5" heading should be reworded, because under 2017 numbering 8.4 is Advance Warning. The details of FIDIC Clauses 9-12 (Tests on Completion, Taking Over, Defects, Tests after Completion) are unverified background and need the Silver Book text.

## Images cover plant rooms well and liquid cooling poorly

The 42 primary candidates map cleanly onto Volumes 2-4. Examples include CC0 generators and cooling towers at a Mesa, AZ data centre, about 50 photos in a consistent CC BY-SA 3.0 power-room set by a single photographer, CC0 FM-200 cylinders at NERSC, and CC BY 2.0 ORNL Frontier cabinets ([Category: Data center power rooms](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Data_center_power_rooms); [FM-200 at NERSC](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:FM-200_fire_suppression_system_at_NERSC.jpg)). The register also flags three reasons for caution. First, NREL gallery photos are under a revocable custom licence that is incompatible with CC BY-SA ([NLR agreement](https://www.nlr.gov/news/image-gallery-user-agreement)), so the "PD-USGov-DOE" Peregrine image stays a doubtful candidate until someone confirms the photographer was a federal employee. Second, OCP media have no single clear licence. Third, brand marks and identifiable people are common, and no frame has been viewed. For CDUs, cold plates, RDHx, lithium-ion UPS racks and in-hall busway, commissioned CC BY-SA line drawings will serve the book better than near-substitute photos.

## Conclusion

The research shows that the book's main exposure is citation drift, not missing topics. A careful reader will lose confidence in numbers that cite the wrong publisher (Ponemon presented as Uptime, NRM2 cited for lightning geometry, NFPA 75 cited for clean-agent hold time) or that conflict with every accessible benchmark (8-12% commissioning cost). Fixing those passages first costs little and protects the book's claim to be practitioner-grade. The 2026 market data supports a method rather than a number: three reputable publishers sit between USD 11 and 18 per watt because they measure different scopes. Teaching normalisation by scope is the more durable contribution, because every headline figure will be out of date within a year. The emerging topics share one pattern. Vendors quote percentage savings, but codes and contracts are what change the BOQ. NFPA 855 thresholds, EnEfG reuse factors, OCP sidecar boundaries and FAT payment milestones are what a QS measures. The book should build its new sections around those and give vendor claims an explicit [VENDOR] tag.
